\begin{abstract}
This paper details our submission for SemEval-2026 Task 6: CLARITY – Unmasking Political Question Evasions. The goal of the task is to use political interviews and classify question–answer pairs from them using a two-level hierarchical taxonomy. The aim is to have each response classified by its high-level “clarity” and its specific fine-grained “evasion technique.” Below we present novel methodologies to build up the baseline models from \citet{b4} and to improve classification performance on this task.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In high-stakes public discourse, particularly in political interviews, the clarity of a response is often as important as its content. The phenomenon of ``equivocation'' or ``evasion'' (the act of responding to a question without directly answering it) is a well-documented strategy used to navigate difficult topics, deflect scrutiny, or manage public perception. While this has been extensively studied in political science and linguistics, the automated detection and classification of such ambiguous responses present a complex challenge for Natural Language Processing (NLP).

This challenge was formally framed as a discrete NLP task by \citet{b4} in their paper: ``I Never Said That'': A dataset, taxonomy and baselines on response clarity classification. The central research question defined by \citet{b4} is: \textbf{How can we automatically and systematically evaluate the clarity of a given response with respect to its corresponding question?}

To address this, a novel two-level hierarchical taxonomy was developed to capture the nuances of response clarity, as shown in Fig \ref{fig:taxonomy}. The first level, Clarity, provides a high-level evaluation of the response's overall directness and completeness in relation to the question. The second level, Evasion, identifies the specific fine-grained linguistic technique or strategy used by the speaker.

Subtask1 and Subtask 2 of SemEval-2026 Task 6 focus on predicting the Clarity and Evasion levels, respectively, for given question-answer pairs from political interviews. The explanation of the taxonomy label for each level is as follows:

\textbf{Clarity Level Categories:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clear Reply:} The information requested is explicitly provided.
    \item \textbf{Clear Non-Reply:} The information requested is not given at all.
    \item \textbf{Ambivalent Reply:} The information requested is given in an incomplete way.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Evasion Level Categories:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Explicit:} The information requested is explicitly stated (in the requested form).
    \item \textbf{Implicit:} The information requested is given, but without being explicitly stated (not in the expected form).
    \item \textbf{General:} The information provided is too general or lacks the requested specificity.
    \item \textbf{Partial:} Offers only a specific component of the requested information.
    \item \textbf{Dodging:} Ignores the question altogether.
    \item \textbf{Deflection:} Starts on topic but shifts the focus and makes a different point than what is asked.
    \item \textbf{Declining to answer:} Acknowledges the question but directly or indirectly refuses to answer at the moment.
    \item \textbf{Claims ignorance:} The answerer claims or admits not to know the answer themselves.
    \item \textbf{Clarification:} Does not provide the requested information and instead asks for clarification.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

The important studies that have influenced our methodology are highlighted in this section.

Examining ambiguous question identification in LLM based QA, \citet{b1} note that models often produce coherent but multi faceted responses, a pattern that also characterizes political evasiveness. To detect ambiguity, their approach uses self consistency prompting to elicit multiple responses, followed by a lightweight Random Forest that assesses variance across those responses. This approach outperforms larger transformer baselines and prompt based methods, particularly in low resource settings. Since political evasion frequently relies on subtle ambiguity, their findings are especially relevant, and their demonstrated effectiveness with limited labeled data aligns well with real world political constraints.

\cite{b2} use the AMBIENT benchmark, a linguist annotated dataset of lexical, syntactic, and pragmatic ambiguities that includes political claims, to evaluate how well language models handle ambiguity. They find that even advanced models such as GPT-4 struggle to distinguish meanings at a human level. However, refined multilabel NLI models can successfully identify claims that are ambiguous or deceptive in the wild. This insight is important for our project because political evasion often relies on ambiguity, and the limitations of existing models highlight the need for domain specific detection techniques.

\citet{b3} survey the use of deep learning in political text analysis, tracing the shift from feature-engineered models to contextual transformers such as BERT. They show that deep models capture nuance and context, essential for interpreting political communication, where subtext and intent are key. Their discussion of transfer learning, annotation efficiency, and ethical issues underscores the importance of scalable and transparent systems for tasks like evasion detection.

While the previously mentioned studies focus on computational ambiguity detection, a separate body of work in linguistics and political science provides the theoretical foundation for evasion taxonomies, like the one used in this SemEval-2026 task. These studies deconstruct the act of ``not answering'' into a set of discrete, observable rhetorical strategies.

For example, \citet{b5} define ``shifting'' as a subtle evasion where the interviewee seemingly accepts the question but ``refocuses'' the question replacing its critical aspect with a more favorable one, a direct analogue to the ``Deflection'' label. They, along with \citet{b6}, identify common patterns like shifts in time, agent, or abstraction level, as well as ``Redirection'' and ``Non-answers''.

\citet{b6} provide the formal pragmatic theory that unites these observations: evasion functions by flouting Grice's Maxims. This framework provides a mechanism for understanding the SemEval-2026 taxonomy: \begin{itemize} \item A response labeled ``Dodging'' or ``Deflection'' is fundamentally flouting the Maxim of Relation (relevance). \item A response labeled ``General'' or ``Partial'' is flouting the Maxim of Quantity (informativeness). \end{itemize}

Other studies provide granular, linguistic-level examples that somewhat map to our labels. \citet{b7} ``non-type-conforming answers'' (e.g., giving a general description instead of a specific number)  aligns  with the ``General'' label. Similarly, Abidin et al. identify ``minimizing the divergence'' (e.g., calling an incident an ``isolated case''), which fits the ``Partial'' or ``General'' labels. These works also categorize confrontational tactics like ``attacking the questioner'' or ``challenging professionalism'', which are forms of ``Dodging'' or ``Deflection''.

Although these papers do not offer models or annotated data, they ground the SemEval-2026 taxonomy in established linguistic theory. Their insights could inform feature engineering or data augmentation, and they help validate the taxonomy’s relevance for capturing political evasion.

The primary computational constraint for sQA pairs is that traditional classifiers like BERT are limited to 512 tokens, necessitating the truncation of long answers and potentially omitting the part containing the evasion \citet{b8}. We address this limitation using ModernBERT, which features a native 8192 sequence length. This expanded context window allows our model to process the entire sQA pair, capturing the full context of long, complex answers.

Lastly, \citet{b9} introduce a useful domain pre-adaptation strategy using their 58,343 Canadian Question Period exchanges. They train a Sentence-BERT model with a self-supervised cosine-similarity objective to learn Q\&A  quality. We can similarly pre-train our classifier on their political Q\&A data to capture domain-specific discourse patterns before fine-tuning on the SemEval-2026 evasion task, potentially improving performance.

\section{Dataset}

The data was sourced from 287 unique interview transcripts of US presidents from 2006 to 2023. The transcripts were taken directly from the official White House website.

A significant challenge identified by the authors was the prevalence of ``multi-barrelled'' questions, where interviewers pose multiple, distinct questions within a single turn. They noted that this complicates the annotation, as a politician might clearly answer one part of the question while evading another.

To address this, they adopted a fine-grained decomposition strategy. First, ChatGPT was leveraged to parse the transcripts and break down these complex, multi-part questions into their constituent ``singular QA pairs'' (sQAs). Each sQA pair consists of a single subquestion from the multi-barrelled question and the complete response from the politician, to determine how that particular question was answered in the context of the full response. An example of this decomposition is shown below:

\begin{quote}
\small % Makes the example text slightly smaller
\textbf{Original Multi-barrelled Question:} \\
\textit{Q. Is there a reason your press staff was unaware of that? And what did you say to the Chinese President?}

\smallskip % Adds a small vertical space
\textbf{Original Full Answer:} \\
\textit{Well—and they weren't with me the entire time. Look, I made it clear that I thought that China had an obligation to be more forthcoming on exactly what the source of the virus was and where it came from.Yes.}

\smallskip

\textbf{Resulting sQA Pair 1:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sub-question:} \textit{Is there a reason your press staff was unaware of that?}
    \item \textbf{Full Answer:} \textit{Well—and they weren't with me the entire time. Look, I made it clear that I thought that China had an obligation to be more forthcoming on exactly what the source of the virus was and where it came from.Yes.}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Resulting sQA Pair 2:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sub-question:} \textit{What did you say to the Chinese President?}
    \item \textbf{Full Answer:} \textit{Well—and they weren't with me the entire time. Look, I made it clear that I thought that China had an obligation to be more forthcoming on exactly what the source of the virus was and where it came from.Yes.}
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}

The resulting dataset had two splits: a training set with 3,450 rows and a test set with 308 rows, with the important columns being ``interview\_question'', ``interview\_answer'', ``question'', ``clarity\_label'', and ``evasion\_label''.

We noted that the evasion\_label column was empty in the test set. The researchers clarified this was intentional. They found that annotators had disagreements (high variance) when assigning these labels, so they decided against using a single ``ground truth'' label.

Instead, the test set provides the individual labels from three annotators in the ``annotator1'', ``annotator2'', and ``annotator3'' columns. For evasion level prediction, a model's prediction is considered correct if it matches any of these three labels.

\section{Methodology}

We propose four distinct architectural approaches to address the CLARITY task: Direct Classification with long-context transformers, Generative Large Language Models (LLMs), Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) with Attention Pooling, and a Natural Language Inference (NLI) re-framing. Additionally, we explore various data augmentation strategies to mitigate the dataset's inherent class imbalance.

\subsection{Task Formulation and Evaluation}
Given a question $Q$ and an answer $A$, the goal is to predict the clarity label (Subtask 1) and the evasion label (Subtask 2). Following the SemEval task guidelines, we utilize the Macro-F1 score as our primary evaluation metric. Due to the significant class imbalance identified in the dataset \citep{b4}, this metric is well-suited for the task. To further address this imbalance during training, we experimented with three distinct loss functions across all models: Focal Loss, Weighted Cross-Entropy, and Dice Loss.

\subsection{Direct Classification (ModernBERT)}
A primary limitation of standard BERT baselines is the 512-token sequence limit, which often necessitates truncating long political responses \citep{b8}. To preserve the full context of the sQA pairs, we utilized ModernBERT \citep{b8}, which supports a native context length of 8192 tokens. We fine-tuned both \texttt{modernbert-base} and \texttt{modernbert-large} by concatenating the question and answer with a separator token $[SEP]$ and feeding the final hidden state of the $[CLS]$ token into a classification head.

\subsection{Multiple Instance Learning (MIL)}
To address the input length constraint without relying solely on newer architectures, we implemented a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) framework with Attention Pooling. This approach is particularly relevant for Subtask 2, where the specific ``evasion technique" (e.g., a deflection) may occur in a small segment of a long answer.

We split the long answers into overlapping segments, or instances, such that for an input pair $(Q, A)$, the answer $A$ forms a bag of instances $\mathcal{I} = \{I_1, I_2, \dots, I_k\}$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input Formatting:} Each instance $I_i$ was constructed by concatenating the full question $Q$ and an answer segment $A_{\text{segment}}$ using the standard BERT format: $I_i = [\texttt{CLS}] \ Q \ [\texttt{SEP}] \ A_{\text{segment}} \ [\texttt{SEP}]$.
    \item \textbf{Chunking Parameters:} The maximum sequence length for each chunk input was set to $\mathbf{256}$ tokens. We used an overlap (stride) of $\mathbf{160}$ tokens, limiting the total number of chunks to a maximum of $\mathbf{16}$ (i.e., $k \le 16$).
    \item \textbf{Attention Pooling:} Each instance $I_i$ was processed independently by the encoder to extract a feature vector, $\mathbf{h}_i$ (the final hidden state of the $[\texttt{CLS}]$ token). These vectors were aggregated using a learnable Attention Layer, which calculates weights ($\alpha_i$) to produce a single weighted context vector $\mathbf{c} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \alpha_i \mathbf{h}_i$ for final classification.
\end{itemize}

Each segment was processed independently by a frozen encoder (we experimented with BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa, and ALBERT) to extract feature vectors. These vectors were aggregated using a learnable Attention Layer, allowing the model to weigh specific segments of the answer more heavily before making a final classification.

\subsection{Natural Language Inference (NLI)}
For Subtask 1 (Clarity Prediction), we hypothesized that the concept of ``clarity" is fundamentally related to logical entailment. We effectively reframed the classification task into a three-way Natural Language Inference (NLI) problem. For every Question-Answer pair $(Q, A)$, we constructed an NLI input consisting of a Premise and a Hypothesis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Premise ($P$):} The politician's full answer $\{A\}$.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis ($H$):} ``The speaker explicitly answers the Question: $\{Q\}$."
\end{itemize}

We utilized the specialized NLI model, \texttt{MoritzLaurer/\allowbreak DeBERTa-v3-large-\allowbreak mnli-fever-anli-ling-wanli}, which is pre-trained on various NLI and factual consistency tasks. A key advantage of this model is its capability to handle longer sequences, which we exploited by setting the maximum input sequence length to $\mathbf{2048}$ tokens.

The resulting NLI predictions were mapped directly to the three clarity labels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Entailment} $\rightarrow$ Clear Reply
    \item \textbf{Contradiction} $\rightarrow$ Clear Non-Reply
    \item \textbf{Neutral} $\rightarrow$ Ambivalent Reply
\end{itemize}

This NLI framing worked well for Subtask 1 because its three output classes aligned directly with the three clarity labels. We did not extend it to Subtask 2, which involves nine evasion categories, because there is no coherent way to design a single premise–hypothesis formulation that meaningfully maps onto such a large, diverse label set.

\subsection{Generative Approach}
We explored the capabilities of generative LLMs by fine-tuning the \texttt{Llama-3.2-3B} instruct model. Due to computational constraints, we used 4-bit quantization (QLoRA). The inputs consisted of each question--answer pair provided directly to the model, and it was prompted to generate the classification label. For training stability and efficiency, we applied LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) with an \textbf{r} value of \textbf{16} and an \textbf{alpha} of \textbf{32}. 

\subsection{Data Augmentation}
To improve robustness, we experimented with five augmentation techniques: Random Word Deletion, Synonym Replacement, Oversampling, Contextual Word Replacement, and Back-Translation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Word Deletion:} We utilized the \texttt{nlpaug} library with a random word deletion action. This method randomly deleted a fixed percentage of words in the answer text, generating augmented samples equivalent to $25\%$ of the original count for each of the three clarity classes.
    \item \textbf{Synonym Replacement:} Using the \texttt{nlpaug} library and the WordNet corpus, words were randomly replaced with their synonyms with a probability of $20\%$ ($\text{aug\_p}=0.2$). This technique was used to generate augmented samples equal to $25\%$ of the original training set size for each clarity class.
    \item \textbf{Oversampling (Weighted Random Sampling):} This technique was applied to address class imbalance by ensuring all classes contributed equally to the training process. Instead of repeating rare samples, we calculated a sampling weight for each instance in the training data based on the inverse frequency of its class. This weighting scheme made the model effectively sample rare classes more frequently during each training epoch.
    \item \textbf{Contextual Word Replacement:} We utilized the \texttt{nlpaug} library with a Masked Language Model (BERT) to mask and replace words based on context.
    \item \textbf{Back-Translation:} Using the Gemini 2.5 Flash API, we translated answers from English to German and back to English. We tested two strategies: augmenting a flat $25\%$ of all classes, and a ``Hard Example" strategy where we identified consistently misclassified samples via 5-fold cross-validation and selectively augmented them.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison with State-of-the-Art}
A critical finding of our work is the efficiency-performance trade-off compared to the researchers' baseline. The baseline employed a massive Llama-2-70B model requiring a 4$\times$A100 GPU cluster (160GB VRAM) to achieve an F1 of 0.68 on Subtask 1.

In contrast, our NLI-based approach using DeBERTa-v3-large (approx. 400M parameters) achieved a superior F1 score of \textbf{0.72} (+4\%). This demonstrates that for the specific task of clarity detection, framing the problem as logical entailment is significantly more effective and computationally efficient than fine-tuning massive generative models. We achieved SOTA-level results on consumer-grade hardware, whereas the baseline required industrial-grade compute.

However, for Subtask 2 (Evasion Techniques), the researchers' baseline (0.51) outperformed our best MIL approach (0.46). This suggests that identifying fine-grained linguistic nuances (e.g., distinguishing ``Deflection" from ``Dodging") benefits from the vast world knowledge and reasoning capabilities embedded in 70B+ parameter models, which smaller discriminative models struggle to capture fully.

\subsection{Performance Analysis}

\subsubsection{Context vs. Segmentation in Evasion}
For Subtask 2, the Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) approach outperformed ModernBERT (0.46 vs. 0.38). While ModernBERT captures the entire context (8192 tokens), the ``evasion" label is often localized. The global pooling of ModernBERT may dilute these specific signals. In contrast, the Attention Pooling mechanism in MIL explicitly assigns high weights to the most discriminative segments of the answer, effectively isolating the evasion tactic.

\subsubsection{The Generative Lag}
Our generative approach (\texttt{Llama-3.2-3B}) underperformed, achieving only 0.57 F1. We observed early overfitting, with performance dropping from 0.57 to 0.50 between epochs. This reinforces our finding that without the massive scale of the researchers' $70\text{B}$ model, generative decoding is less stable than discriminative classification for this task.

\subsubsection{Inefficacy of Augmentation on Subtask 1}
In this study, our augmentation experiments were restricted exclusively to Subtask 1 (Clarity). Unfortunately, none of the strategies described in Section 4.6 yielded improvements in F1 scores for clarity prediction. Consequently, we did not extend these specific augmentation pipelines to Subtask 2 (Evasion) for the current experiments.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
A significant limitation was the computational cost of the Generative approach (approx. $\mathbf{8}$ hours/epoch), which severely restricted our ability to tune hyperparameters. We faced considerable challenges with resource allocation, as the long training time often led to Kaggle platform timeouts. Consequently, we were not able to run the model to completion and hyperparameter tuning was severely limited by these compute constraints. Because of the poor score and the computation cost, we did not use the generative approach for Subtask 2.

In future work, we plan to use Synthetic Data Generation as an augmentation strategy. We will prompt a large instruction-tuned model (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Flash API or GPT-5-mini) using a few-shot setup. For each of the 3 Clarity Level classes and 9 Evasion Level classes, we will provide the model with \textbf{5} manually selected question--answer examples representing that class. The model will then be instructed to generate new question--answer pairs that strictly follow the target label, allowing us to create high-quality synthetic samples to expand our training dataset.

We also intend to explore smaller open-source generative LLMs (e.g., Qwen, Mistral) that have shown performance competitive with or exceeding larger models, to assess whether the strategy of using generative LLMs to predict Evasion labels is still feasible or not.

Moreover, even though the data augmentation techniques mentioned in Section 4.6 were not effective for Subtask 1, we will still implement them to counteract the class imbalance of Subtask 2, to determine whether those techniques yield performance improvements for fine-grained evasion detection.
