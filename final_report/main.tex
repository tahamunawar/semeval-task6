\begin{abstract}
This paper details our submission for SemEval-2026 Task 6: CLARITY – Unmasking Political Question Evasions. The goal of the task is to use political interviews and classify question–answer pairs from them using a two-level hierarchical taxonomy. The aim is to have each response classified by its high-level “clarity” and its specific fine-grained “evasion technique.” Below we present novel methodologies to build up the baseline models from \citet{b4} and to improve classification performance on this task. Our best-performing models achieve macro-F1 scores of 0.81 on Subtask 1 and 0.45 on Subtask 2.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In high-stakes public discourse, particularly in political interviews, the clarity of a response is often as important as its content. The phenomenon of ``equivocation'' or ``evasion'' (the act of responding to a question without directly answering it) is a well-documented strategy used to navigate difficult topics, deflect scrutiny, or manage public perception. While this has been extensively studied in political science and linguistics, the automated detection and classification of such ambiguous responses present a complex challenge for Natural Language Processing (NLP).

This challenge was formally framed as a discrete NLP task by \citet{b4} in their paper: ``I Never Said That'': A dataset, taxonomy and baselines on response clarity classification. The central research question defined by \citet{b4} is: \textbf{How can we automatically and systematically evaluate the clarity of a given response with respect to its corresponding question?}

To address this, a novel two-level hierarchical taxonomy was developed to capture the nuances of response clarity, as shown in Fig \ref{fig:taxonomy}. The first level, Clarity, provides a high-level evaluation of the response's overall directness and completeness in relation to the question. The second level, Evasion, identifies the specific fine-grained linguistic technique or strategy used by the speaker.

Our work for SemEval-2026 Task 6 addresses both Subtask 1 and Subtask 2 by predicting labels from the two-level taxonomy.

\section{Literature Review}

Identifying political evasion is challenging because models often produce coherent but multifaceted responses to ambiguous questions \citep{b1}. While advanced models still struggle to distinguish pragmatic meanings at a human level, refined Natural Language Inference (NLI) models show promise in identifying deceptive claims \citep{b2}. In political science, the field has shifted from feature engineering to contextual transformers like BERT to capture the nuance and intent essential for interpreting communication \citep{b3}.

The theoretical basis for evasion, responding without answering, is grounded in rhetorical strategies such as ``shifting,'' where an interviewee refocuses a critical question to a more favorable topic \citep{b5}. This behavior is formally explained as flouting Grice's Maxims; for instance, labels like ``Dodging'' or ``Deflection'' flout the Maxim of Relation (relevance), while ``General'' or ``Partial'' labels flout the Maxim of Quantity (informativeness) \citep{b6, b7}. 

A significant technical constraint in this domain is that traditional encoders are limited to 512 tokens, often forcing the truncation of long, complex political answers and potentially losing the specific segment containing the evasion tactic \citep{b8}. We address this by utilizing ModernBERT \citep{b8}, leveraging its native 8192-token context window to process complete question-answer pairs.

% Lastly, \citet{b9} introduce a useful domain pre-adaptation strategy using their 58,343 Canadian Question Period exchanges. They train a Sentence-BERT model with a self-supervised cosine-similarity objective to learn Q\&A  quality. We can similarly pre-train our classifier on their political Q\&A data to capture domain-specific discourse patterns before fine-tuning on the SemEval-2026 evasion task, potentially improving performance.

\section{Methodology}

We propose six distinct architectural approaches to address the CLARITY task. Additionally, we explore various data augmentation strategies to mitigate the dataset's inherent class imbalance.

\subsection{Task Formulation and Evaluation}
Given a question $Q$ and an answer $A$, the goal is to predict the clarity label (Subtask 1) and the evasion label (Subtask 2). Following the SemEval task guidelines, we utilize the Macro-F1 score as our primary evaluation metric. Due to the significant class imbalance identified in the dataset \citep{b4}, this metric is well-suited for the task. To further address this imbalance during training, we experimented with three distinct loss functions across all models: Focal Loss, Weighted Cross-Entropy, and Dice Loss.

\subsection{Direct Classification (ModernBERT)}
A primary limitation of standard BERT baselines is the 512-token sequence limit, which often necessitates truncating long political responses \citep{b8}. To preserve the full context of the sQA pairs, we utilized ModernBERT \citep{b8}, which supports a native context length of 8192 tokens. We fine-tuned both \texttt{modernbert-base} and \texttt{modernbert-large} by concatenating the question and answer with a separator token $[SEP]$ and feeding the final hidden state of the $[CLS]$ token into a classification head.

\subsection{Multiple Instance Learning (MIL)}
To address the input length constraint without relying solely on newer architectures, we implemented a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) framework with Attention Pooling. This approach is particularly relevant for Subtask 2, where the specific ``evasion technique" (e.g., a deflection) may occur in a small segment of a long answer.

We split the long answers into overlapping segments, or instances, such that for an input pair $(Q, A)$, the answer $A$ forms a bag of instances $\mathcal{I} = \{I_1, I_2, \dots, I_k\}$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input Formatting:} Each instance $I_i$ was constructed by concatenating the full question $Q$ and an answer segment $A_{\text{segment}}$ using the standard BERT format: $I_i = [\texttt{CLS}] \ Q \ [\texttt{SEP}] \ A_{\text{segment}} \ [\texttt{SEP}]$.
    \item \textbf{Chunking Parameters:} The maximum sequence length for each chunk input was set to $\mathbf{256}$ tokens. We used an overlap (stride) of $\mathbf{160}$ tokens, limiting the total number of chunks to a maximum of $\mathbf{16}$ (i.e., $k \le 16$).
    \item \textbf{Attention Pooling:} Each instance $I_i$ was processed independently by the encoder to extract a feature vector, $\mathbf{h}_i$ (the final hidden state of the $[\texttt{CLS}]$ token). These vectors were aggregated using a learnable Attention Layer, which calculates weights ($\alpha_i$) to produce a single weighted context vector $\mathbf{c} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \alpha_i \mathbf{h}_i$ for final classification.
\end{itemize}

Each segment was processed independently by a frozen encoder (we experimented with BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa, and ALBERT) to extract feature vectors. These vectors were aggregated using a learnable Attention Layer, allowing the model to weigh specific segments of the answer more heavily before making a final classification.

\subsection{Natural Language Inference (NLI)}
For Subtask 1 (Clarity Prediction), we hypothesized that the concept of ``clarity" is fundamentally related to logical entailment. We effectively reframed the classification task into a three-way Natural Language Inference (NLI) problem. For every Question-Answer pair $(Q, A)$, we constructed an NLI input consisting of a Premise and a Hypothesis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Premise ($P$):} The politician's full answer $\{A\}$.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis ($H$):} ``The speaker explicitly answers the Question: $\{Q\}$."
\end{itemize}

We used the specialized NLI model \texttt{MoritzLaurer/\allowbreak DeBERTa-v3-large-\allowbreak mnli-fever-anli-ling-wanli}, pre-trained on multiple NLI and factual consistency tasks. While \citet{b4} noted that traditional encoder-only classifiers like BERT are constrained by a 512-token limit, we extended the input length to $\mathbf{2048}$ tokens by leveraging DeBERTa-v3’s relative position embeddings, which generalize to longer sequences given sufficient memory, enabling full-context processing of long political interviews.

The resulting NLI predictions were mapped directly to the three clarity labels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Entailment} $\rightarrow$ Clear Reply
    \item \textbf{Contradiction} $\rightarrow$ Clear Non-Reply
    \item \textbf{Neutral} $\rightarrow$ Ambivalent Reply
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Adversarial Weight Perturbation (AWP)}
To improve generalization and robustness against the varied phrasing of political responses, we implemented AWP. AWP regularizes the model by seeking a flatter minimum in the loss landscape through calculated noise. During the backward pass, we calculate an adversarial perturbation $\delta$ for the model weights $\theta$ by maximizing the loss:
\begin{equation*}
\theta_{adv} = \theta + \epsilon \frac{\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)}{\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)\|_2}
\end{equation*}
where $\epsilon$ is set to $10^{-3}$ starting from the second epoch. This forces the model to learn semantic features that are invariant to small shifts in parameter space, mitigating overfitting on hard examples.

\subsubsection{Hybrid Probability Calibration and Blending}
To reconcile biases between different calibration methods, we implemented a Weighted Probability Blend (Soft Voting). We utilized Isotonic Regression ($P_{iso}$) to act as a conservative estimator and Temperature Scaling ($P_{ts}$) to serve as a liberal estimator by softening probability distributions. The final probability distribution $P_{final}$ is the weighted average of the two calibrated distributions:
$$P_{final} = w_{iso} P_{iso} + (1 - w_{iso}) P_{ts}$$

This NLI framing worked well for Subtask 1 because its three output classes aligned directly with the three clarity labels. We did not extend it to Subtask 2, which involves nine evasion categories, because there is no coherent way to design a single premise–hypothesis formulation that meaningfully maps onto such a large, diverse label set.

\subsection{Generative Approach}
We explored the capabilities of generative LLMs by fine-tuning the \texttt{Llama-3.2-3B} instruct model. Due to computational constraints, we used 4-bit quantization (QLoRA). The inputs consisted of each question--answer pair provided directly to the model, and it was prompted to generate the classification label. For training stability and efficiency, we applied LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) with an \textbf{r} value of \textbf{16} and an \textbf{alpha} of \textbf{32}. 

\subsection{Multi-Task Hierarchical Gating (MTL)}
We implemented a Multi-Task Learning (MTL) architecture using ModernBERT-large to jointly optimize for Clarity (parent) and Evasion (child) labels. This approach explicitly models the taxonomic dependencies between the two subtasks.

\subsubsection{Architectural Gating}

The model utilizes a hierarchical gating mechanism to modulate information flow. A shared representation $h \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is generated via mean pooling of the final hidden states. The Clarity Head first computes the probability distribution $P(c|h)$. This distribution is projected into a gating vector $g$, which performs element-wise multiplication on the original representation to isolate features relevant to the predicted clarity level:

\begin{equation*}
    g = \sigma(W_g P(c|h) + b_g), \quad h_{gated} = h \odot g
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Consistency Loss}

To ensure the model respects the logical structure of the taxonomy, where a fine-grained evasion technique must belong to its high-level clarity category, we introduced a Consistency Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{cons}$). This term penalizes the model if the probability of a child label $P(e)$ exceeds the probability of its parent clarity label $P(c_{M(e)})$:

\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{L}_{cons} = \sum_{i} \text{softplus}(P(e_i) - P(c_{M(e_i)}))
\end{equation*}

The final training objective is a weighted sum: 
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{L}_{total} = \alpha \mathcal{L}_{evasion} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{clarity} + \gamma \mathcal{L}_{cons}
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Hierarchy-Aware Global Model (HiAGM)}
To explicitly capture the taxonomic dependencies between clarity levels and evasion techniques, we implemented a Hierarchy-Aware Global Model (HiAGM). Unlike flat classifiers, HiAGM treats the labels as a directed graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$, where $\mathcal{V}$ represents the set of all nodes (Root, Clarity, and Evasion)

\subsubsection{Label Graph Convolution}
We define the structural relationship of the taxonomy using a normalized adjacency matrix $\tilde{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times |\mathcal{V}|}$ with self-loops. We utilize a single-layer Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) with Xavier initialization to learn hierarchy-aware label embeddings:
$$W_{labels} = \text{ReLU}(\tilde{A} \cdot E \cdot W_g)$$
where $E \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times 768}$ is the initial label embedding matrix and $W_g$ is the weight matrix. This allows the model to propagate features from parent clarity labels to child evasion techniques

\subsubsection{ModernBERT Encoder and Fusion}
We use ModernBERT-base as the text encoder, utilizing a sequence length of 1024 tokens to capture long-form political discourse. Given a text representation $h \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ from the [CLS] token, the final logits are computed via dot-product compatibility:
$$\text{Logits} = h \cdot (W_{labels})^\top$$
The model is optimized using Focal Loss ($\gamma=3.0$) with class weights to mitigate the severe label imbalance. To ensure structural integrity, Subtask 1 labels are not predicted independently; instead, they are deterministically inferred post-hoc from the predicted Subtask 2 child node, enforcing 100\% hierarchical consistency.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}
To improve robustness, we experimented with the following augmentation techniques: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Word Deletion:} We utilized the \texttt{nlpaug} library with a random word deletion action. This method randomly deleted a fixed percentage of words in the answer text, generating augmented samples equivalent to $25\%$ of the original count for each of the three clarity classes.
    % \item \textbf{Synonym Replacement:} Using the \texttt{nlpaug} library and the WordNet corpus, words were randomly replaced with their synonyms with a probability of $20\%$ ($\text{aug\_p}=0.2$). This technique was used to generate augmented samples equal to $25\%$ of the original training set size for each clarity class.
    \item \textbf{Oversampling (Weighted Random Sampling):} To address class imbalance, we assigned each training instance a sampling weight based on the inverse frequency of its class, ensuring equal class contribution during training and more frequent sampling of rare classes without explicit duplication.
    \item \textbf{Contextual Word Replacement:} We utilized the \texttt{nlpaug} library with a Masked Language Model (BERT) to mask and replace words based on context.
    \item \textbf{Back-Translation:} Using the Gemini 2.5 Flash API, we translated answers from English to German and back to English. We identified consistently misclassified samples via 5-fold cross-validation on the train set and selectively augmented them.
    \item \textbf{Few-Shot Synthetic Generation:} We prompted Gemini 2.5 Flash with five manually selected examples of specific minority classes and instructed it to generate entirely new, logically consistent question-answer pairs.
    % \item \textbf{Cross-Class Paraphrasing:} We experimented with a transformation strategy where the model was given an existing response and tasked with paraphrasing it to fit a different target evasion category while maintaining the original topic.
\end{itemize}

These techniques were applied across both subtasks to address class imbalance.

\subsection{Comparison with Benchmark}
A critical finding of our work is the efficiency-performance trade-off compared to the researchers' baseline. The baseline employed a massive Llama-2-70B model requiring a 4$\times$A100 GPU cluster (160GB VRAM) to achieve an F1 of 0.68 on Subtask 1.

In contrast, our NLI-based approach using DeBERTa-v3-large (approx. 400M parameters) achieved a final evaluation F1 score of \textbf{0.81}. This demonstrates that for the specific task of clarity detection, framing the problem as logical entailment is significantly more effective and computationally efficient than fine-tuning massive generative models. 

However, for Subtask 2 (Evasion Techniques), the researchers' baseline (0.51) outperformed our best MIL approach \textbf{0.45}. This suggests that identifying fine-grained linguistic nuances (e.g., distinguishing ``Deflection" from ``Dodging") benefits from the vast world knowledge and reasoning capabilities embedded in 70B+ parameter models, which smaller discriminative models struggle to capture fully.

\subsection{Performance Analysis}

\subsubsection{Context vs. Segmentation in Evasion}
For Subtask 2, the Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) approach outperformed ModernBERT (0.46 vs. 0.38). While ModernBERT captures the entire context (8192 tokens), the ``evasion" label is often localized. The global pooling of ModernBERT may dilute these specific signals. In contrast, the Attention Pooling mechanism in MIL explicitly assigns high weights to the most discriminative segments of the answer, effectively isolating the evasion tactic.

\subsubsection{The Trade-off of Hierarchical Constraints}
While the Multi-Task Hierarchical Gating (MTL) and Hierarchy-Aware Global Model (HiAGM) were designed to explicitly model the taxonomic dependencies described in Section 4.6 and 4.7 , they achieved lower Macro-F1 scores (0.64 and 0.52 respectively) on Subtask 1 compared to the NLI approach. We observe that the Hierarchical Consistency Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{cons}$) and the post-hoc deterministic inference used in HiAGM  may have been overly restrictive. By forcing the model to strictly adhere to the parent-child relationship, we likely introduced a "bottleneck" where errors in fine-grained evasion detection (Subtask 2) propagated upward, penalizing the clarity prediction (Subtask 1). In contrast, the NLI model's flexibility to treat clarity as a logical entailment problem allowed for better generalization across varied presidential rhetorical styles.

\subsubsection{The Generative Lag}
Our generative approach (\texttt{Llama-3.2-3B}) underperformed, achieving only 0.57 F1. We observed early overfitting, with performance dropping from 0.57 to 0.50 between epochs. This reinforces our finding that without the massive scale of the researchers' $70\text{B}$ model, generative decoding is less stable than discriminative classification for this task.

\subsubsection{Inefficacy of Augmentation}
Contrary to our hypothesis, the data augmentation strategies described in Section 4.8 did not yield statistically significant improvements in Macro-F1 scores for either Subtask 1 or Subtask 2, with performance remaining stagnant or declining.

We attribute this to the \textit{fragility of political nuance}. Political evasion depends on subtle rhetorical cues (e.g., ``shifting'' or ``flouting Gricean Maxims''), making class boundaries highly sensitive to perturbations. This is consistent with \citet{b4}, who reported low inter-annotator agreement ($\kappa = 0.48$) and difficulty distinguishing semantically proximate categories such as \textit{General} and \textit{Implicit}. Such inherent ambiguity likely caused both lexical augmentation and LLM-generated data to introduce noise rather than useful diversity, explaining the lack of performance gains.

\subsubsection{NLI Calibration and Error Analysis}
Initial experiments revealed significant semantic overlap between the \textit{Clear Reply} and \textit{Ambivalent} classes, leading to a performance bottleneck. This aligns with \citet{b4}, who reported that annotators struggled most to distinguish between the parent categories \textit{Clear Reply} and \textit{Ambivalent} in the clarity dimension, with inter-annotator agreement dropping to $\kappa = 0.65$, compared to much higher agreement for other high-level distinctions. The introduction of Adversarial Weight Perturbation (AWP) proved vital, seeking a flatter loss landscape that improved the macro-F1 from 0.70 to 0.74 by enhancing model robustness against varied political phrasing. Building on this, our hybrid post-training probability calibration further decoupled the remaining semantic overlap, resulting in our final state-of-the-art macro-F1 score of 0.79. Our analysis showed that the distinct calibration methods yielded specific performance biases: Isotonic Regression ($P_{iso}$) provided high precision (0.84) for clear replies, while Temperature Scaling ($P_{ts}$) recovered recall (0.78). Through a grid search on the validation set, we determined the optimal temperature to be $T = 1.23$ and the optimal weighting factor to be $w_{iso} = 0.59$. 


\section{Limitations}
A primary challenge in our experimentation was the significant computational disparity between our infrastructure and the task's 70B-parameter baseline. Our generative fine-tuning was restricted by Kaggle and Google Colab runtime limits, where the 8-hour-per-epoch training cycle frequently triggered platform timeouts and session disconnections. These compute constraints prevented us from performing exhaustive hyperparameter tuning or completing longer training runs, which likely contributed to the 'generative lag' observed in our results.
